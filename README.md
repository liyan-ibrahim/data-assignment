For this assignment, I decided to work with a Scooby-Doo dataset to explore how animated shows represent nonhuman characters (monsters) and their roles within a story. Since my teams children's show that we're developing explores themes of identity, belonging, and finding connection beyond the human, I thought Scooby-Doo would be an interesting reference point (and this dataset has a lot of info so it's fun to analyze).
</br> 
I wanted to focus on real vs. fake monsters over time, analyzing how often the monsters in Scooby-Doo are genuinely supernatural versus humans in disguise across different decades.
</br> 
I organized the data by decade, calculated the percentages of real and fake monsters, and visualized the results in a stacked bar chart.
</br> 
I was curious to see whether Scooby-Doo has changed how it portrays monsters over the years. The results showed that real monsters were much more common in certain decades, particularly the 1980s, while more recent episodes tended to feature fake monsters. 
</br> 
I then went down a rabbit hole of figuring out why this might be. When Scooby-Doo, Where Are You! premiered in 1969, the central premise was that apparently supernatural monsters were usually ordinary people wearing disguises. But in the early 1980s, producers began experimenting with the show's format.
</br> 
But then I looked at the 1980s more broadly and realized this was also a huge period for fantasy and supernatural cartoons. Shows like He-Man, ThunderCats, and The Real Ghostbusters were becoming popular, and there seemed to be a growing interest in magical worlds, strange creatures, and stories where the supernatural was actually real.
</br> 
I also found that Scooby-Doo had introduced shows like The 13 Ghosts of Scooby-Doo in 1985, where the ghosts weren't people in costumes anymore. They were actual ghosts.
